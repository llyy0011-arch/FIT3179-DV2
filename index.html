THE BIGGER PICTURE

Beyond the National Average

In 2022, approximately 5.5 million Australians were living with disability, representing 21.4% of the population. However, these national figures do not reveal how disability differs between demographic groups or how access to support varies across Australia.

This story begins with who experiences disability, then follows the distribution of NDIS participation and daily assistance needs. It examines the role of formal and informal support before turning to healthcare use, unmet specialist needs and barriers to GP access.

01

THE NATIONAL PICTURE

Who Experiences Disability?

Disability prevalence is not uniform across the Australian population. Age and sex provide two important perspectives for understanding differences hidden within the national average.

DISABILITY PREVALENCE

How Does Disability Differ Across Age Groups?

Australia's overall disability prevalence was 21.4% in 2022. However, prevalence increases substantially with age, with older Australians experiencing much higher rates than younger age groups. The chart also reveals differences between males and females.

CHANGES BETWEEN 2003 AND 2022

How Has Disability Prevalence Changed Over Time?

Disability prevalence has changed differently across age groups between 2003 and 2022. The dumbbell chart shows that the direction and magnitude of change vary across generations. While some younger age groups experienced increases in disability prevalence, some older age groups recorded declines.

Understanding who experiences disability is only the beginning. The next question is how disability support and assistance needs are distributed across Australia.

DAILY ASSISTANCE NEEDS

Where Is the Need for Daily Assistance Greatest?

NDIS participation does not capture everyone who needs assistance with everyday activities. The 2021 Australian Census recorded approximately 1.46 million people who needed assistance with core activities, including self-care, mobility and communication.

New South Wales recorded the largest number of people needing assistance, while Tasmania had the highest proportion relative to its population. This contrast highlights the difference between absolute demand and relative prevalence.

FORMAL AND INFORMAL ASSISTANCE

Who Provides Essential Disability Assistance?

For Australians with profound or severe core activity limitations, informal assistance is more common than formal assistance across all six activities. Mobility (76.5%) has the highest rate of informal assistance, followed by meal preparation (70.2%) and communication (67.3%) .

The largest gap occurs in meal preparation, where 70.2% receive informal assistance compared with only 17.2% who receive formal assistance, a difference of 53 percentage points. This highlights the important role of informal carers in everyday disability support.

Disability support includes both formal services and informal help. Yet assistance with daily activities is only one part of the story. The next section explores whether people with disability can access the healthcare they need.

03

ACCESS AND BARRIERS

When Healthcare Is Needed, Can People Access It?

Using a health service does not necessarily mean every healthcare need is met. Begin with patterns of service use, then compare access to medical specialists across regions. Finally, explore why people miss GP care and how these barriers differ by age and sex.

HEALTHCARE SERVICE ACCESS

How Accessible Are Essential Health Services?

Healthcare service use varies across different age groups and types of care. Seeing a GP for personal health is among the most commonly reported services, with rates exceeding 80% in several age groups. In comparison, hospital admissions are considerably less frequent. This highlights the central role of general practitioners in healthcare for Australians with disability.

Service use provides the starting point, but it does not reveal whether care was available when people needed it. The next chart focuses on unmet medical specialist needs across metropolitan and regional Australia.

SPECIALIST CARE AND REMOTENESS

Does Location Affect Access to Medical Specialists?

In 2022, 14.4% of people with disability in major cities needed to see a medical specialist but did not, compared with 13.0% in both inner regional and outer regional or remote areas. The chart also shows differences in the share who saw a specialist when needed and who did not need specialist care.

Differences in access to specialist care raise a broader question: what prevents people from receiving healthcare? GP services offer a closer look at the barriers people experience and the groups most affected.

UNMET HEALTHCARE NEEDS

What Prevents People from Seeing a GP?

Waiting times are a major obstacle to accessing GP care among people with disability. Across the population examined, approximately 43.1% reported waiting times as a reason for unmet GP needs, compared with 8.8% who reported cost. Among people with severe or profound disability, waiting times accounted for 41.0%, while cost accounted for 9.0%.

AGE DIFFERENCES

Does Age Change the Experience of GP Access?

Waiting times remain a prominent barrier across age groups, but their relative importance differs by age. They account for approximately 47.7% among people aged 45 to 64 and 47.2% among those aged 65 and over, compared with 37.0% among people under 25. Cost-related barriers also differ across age groups.

COST AND GENDER INEQUALITY

Who Is Most Affected by the Cost of GP Care?

Among people with disability aged 25 to 44, 22.6% of females did not see a GP when needed because of cost, compared with 14.2% of males . This represents a gap of 8.4 percentage points .

The pattern also appears among those aged 45 to 64, where 13.2% of females reported cost-related unmet GP needs, compared with 8.3% of males. However, cost was not the only barrier. Among females aged 25 to 44, 34.7% reported not seeing a GP because of other reasons.

THE BIGGER PICTURE

Beyond Disability Prevalence

Disability in Australia is not experienced equally. Prevalence differs across age groups, while NDIS participation and assistance needs vary between states and territories. Both formal and informal providers contribute to essential disability support.

However, access to healthcare remains uneven. Differences in specialist care, GP waiting times and financial barriers reveal gaps between healthcare needs and the services people receive. The large gender gap in cost-related GP access among adults aged 25 to 44 highlights how these barriers affect some groups more than others.

Together, these findings highlight the importance of looking beyond national averages to understand where support is needed and where barriers to essential healthcare remain.
